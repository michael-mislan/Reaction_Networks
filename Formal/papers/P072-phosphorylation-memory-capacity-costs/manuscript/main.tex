\title{Cycle structure, capacity, and resource costs\\ of phosphorylation memory}
\author{}

\begin{abstract}
How many recoverable states can a substrate with \(n\) modification sites support when all
sites share one kinase and one phosphatase? We study the distributive mass-action network on
the \(n\)-cube of phosphoforms in which every enzyme--edge interaction has its own bound
complex and all bound substrate is retained in the conserved inventory. Let \(C_n\) be the
largest number of locally asymptotically stable positive equilibria that one list of rate
constants can have in one compatibility class. We prove
\(\max\{n,\lfloor(2^{n-2}+n)/2\rfloor\}\le C_n\le 2^n\), so that \(C_n=\Theta(2^n)\); the upper
bound counts nonhyperbolic attractors, and the lower bound is attained by hyperbolic sinks. In
contrast, when the effective modification ratios are balanced around every elementary square
of the cube, the capacity is exactly \(n\), the value for a single sequential chain; more than
\(n\) stable states therefore require a nonzero effective cycle affinity. The exponential
construction combines an exponentially large loading-interpolation space with an
independently normally attracting saturated family. We then separate this structural capacity
from operation. A finite-molecule theorem for the full continuous-time Markov chain converts
any finite set of hyperbolic sinks into labels with explicit retention, recovery and readout
guarantees at a computable sufficient system size; a local large-deviation theorem for crowded
states gives an exit barrier of order \(\delta^{2n}\), with recovery time of order
\(\delta^{-(2n-2)}\) at separation \(\delta\); and a reversible extension with one common
ATP-hydrolysis potential shows that finitely many sinks persist under finite chemical driving.
Exact certificates for a three-site source with four sinks and seven equilibria illustrate the
theory. For this example the sufficient certificates remain astronomically expensive, and we
prove a concentration-ratio obstruction for its design family; these are diagnoses of the
constructions, not lower bounds for phosphorylation memory in general. The proofs are
conventional mathematics, supported by exact rational and interval computations and by a small
amount of machine-checked algebra.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Background and question}
Reversible phosphorylation at several sites is among the most common mechanisms by which cells
regulate protein function \cite{Cohen2000,SalazarHofer2009}. Since Lisman's proposal that a
bistable kinase--phosphatase system could store information for times far longer than the
lifetime of its molecules \cite{Crick1984,Lisman1985}, multisite modification has also been
discussed as a chemical memory, and the stochastic lifetime of such switches has been studied
in detail for particular models \cite{Bialek2001,MZLW2005}. The mathematical starting point is
multistationarity. Markevich, Hoek and Kholodenko observed that a two-site substrate acted on
distributively by one kinase and one phosphatase can be bistable although its only feedback is
competition for the shared enzymes \cite{Markevich2004}. For the \emph{sequential} mechanism,
in which the sites are modified in a fixed order, Wang and Sontag proved that there are at most
\(2n-1\) positive equilibria \cite{WangSontag2008}; examples with \(2n-1\) equilibria are known
for \(n\le4\) \cite{FHC2014}; and Feliu, Rendall and Wiuf proved that \(\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1\)
of the equilibria can be asymptotically stable \cite{FRW2020}. A companion paper
\cite{Companion} shows that the sequential chain has at most \(n\) locally asymptotically
stable equilibria and that \(n\) hyperbolic sinks, together with \(n-1\) saddles, occur for
every \(n\). Further results on parameter regions, bistability and dynamics are in
\cite{Gunawardena2005,ConradiFlockerziRaisch2008,FeliuWiuf2012,HolsteinFlockerziConradi2013,
ConradiMincheva2014,HellRendall2015,CFMW2017,BihanDickensteinGiaroli2020,FKdWY2022,FKdWY2023,
TorresFeliu2021}, and surveys in \cite{ConradiShiu2018,Dickenstein2016,Dickenstein2020}.

A substrate with \(n\) sites has \(2^n\) phosphoforms, not \(n+1\), and when the order of
modification is not enforced all of them participate
\cite{Gunawardena2007,SuwanmajoKrishnan2015}. Thomson and Gunawardena showed that the
equilibria of such post-translational modification systems are still described by rational
functions of the free-enzyme ratio \cite{ThomsonGunawardena2009b,ManraiGunawardena2008}, and
their numerical experiments suggested that the number of stable phosphoform distributions
grows with \(n\) \cite{ThomsonGunawardena2009}. This raises the question studied here.

\begin{quote}
\emph{For the unordered distributive mechanism with one kinase and one phosphatase, how many
stable equilibria can coexist for one set of rate constants and one set of conserved totals,
which kinetic structure is responsible for the number, and what does it cost to use these
equilibria as the states of a finite-molecule memory?}
\end{quote}

The last part of the question matters because four quantities are easily conflated
(Table~\ref{tab:notions}). An equilibrium can be stable and yet restore disturbances so slowly,
or lie so close to its neighbours, that it is useless as a stored state; and a rigorous
probability bound for a finite number of molecules can be far more expensive than the
underlying dynamics. Throughout this paper the word \emph{capacity} refers to the second row of
Table~\ref{tab:notions} unless it is qualified.

\subsection{The model}
Vertices of the \(n\)-cube are subsets \(I\subseteq[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}\), and \(S_I\) denotes
the phosphoform whose phosphorylated sites are exactly those in \(I\). An \emph{edge} is a
pair \(e=(\tl e,\hd e)\) with \(\hd e=\tl e\cup\{i\}\), \(i\notin\tl e\); we call \(\tl e\) the
tail and \(\hd e\) the head. There are \(q=2^n\) vertices and \(m=n2^{n-1}\) edges. For every
edge the kinase \(E\) and the phosphatase \(F\) act by the distributive mass-action mechanism
\begin{equation}\label{eq:source}
 S_{\tl e}+E\ \underset{b_e}{\overset{a_e}{\rightleftharpoons}}\ C_e
       \ \xrightarrow{\ c_e\ }\ S_{\hd e}+E,
 \qquad
 S_{\hd e}+F\ \underset{\beta_e}{\overset{\alpha_e}{\rightleftharpoons}}\ Y_e
       \ \xrightarrow{\ \gamma_e\ }\ S_{\tl e}+F .
\end{equation}
All \(6m=3n2^n\) rate constants are positive. The two enzymes are shared by all edges, so the
binding reactions compete for the free enzymes, but the complexes \(C_e,Y_e\) are
edge-specific. A mechanism in which one bound complex can release several different products
is a different network, and our results do not automatically transfer to it. There are
\(q+2m+2=(n+1)2^n+2\) species and three conserved totals,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:totals}
 \ET=E+\sum_e C_e,\qquad \FT=F+\sum_eY_e,\qquad
 \ST=\sum_I S_I+\sum_e(C_e+Y_e).
\end{equation}
The bound substrate is part of \(\ST\); no quasi-steady-state or low-enzyme approximation is
made anywhere in the paper. The set of nonnegative concentrations with prescribed positive
totals is a \emph{compatibility class}. It is a compact convex polytope of dimension
\(d=q+2m-1\), invariant under the dynamics, and every statement about stability refers to the
dynamics within a class. For \(n=3\) there are \(34\) species, \(12\) edges, \(72\) rate
constants and \(d=31\). The \emph{sequential chain} is the analogous network on a path
\(S_0,\dots,S_n\) with \(n\) edges \cite{WangSontag2008,Companion}.

\begin{definition}\label{def:capacity}
A \emph{sink} is a positive equilibrium that is locally asymptotically stable relative to its
compatibility class; it need not be hyperbolic. A \emph{hyperbolic sink} is one at which the
Jacobian restricted to the class is Hurwitz. The \emph{stable-equilibrium capacity} \(C_n\) is
the supremum, over all positive rate constants and all positive totals, of the number of sinks
in the class. Equilibria of different rate lists or different classes are never combined.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Results}
Put \(p_e=a_e/(b_e+c_e)\), \(q_e=\alpha_e/(\beta_e+\gamma_e)\) (the \emph{loadings}) and
\(k_e^+=c_ep_e\), \(k_e^-=\gamma_eq_e\) (the \emph{effective rates}), and let
\(\rho_e=k_e^+/k_e^-\). The rates are \emph{square balanced} if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:balance-intro}
 \rho_{(I,I\cup\{i\})}\,\rho_{(I\cup\{i\},I\cup\{i,j\})}
 =\rho_{(I,I\cup\{j\})}\,\rho_{(I\cup\{j\},I\cup\{i,j\})}
\end{equation}
for every \(I\) and every pair \(i\ne j\) outside \(I\); the logarithm of the quotient of the
two sides is the \emph{effective affinity} of that square.

\begin{theorem}[Architecture]\label{thm:capacity}
For every \(n\ge2\),
\[
 \max\Bigl\{n,\Bigl\lfloor\frac{2^{n-2}+n}{2}\Bigr\rfloor\Bigr\}\ \le\ C_n\ \le\ 2^n ,
\]
and \(C_3\ge4\). Consequently \(2^{n-3}\le C_n\le2^n\) for \(n\ge3\), and \(C_n=\Theta(2^n)\).
The upper bound holds for sinks in the sense of Definition~\textup{\ref{def:capacity}},
including nonhyperbolic ones; every lower bound is attained by hyperbolic sinks of
\eqref{eq:source} with one common list of positive rational rate constants and one triple of
positive rational totals.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Balanced ceiling]\label{thm:balanced}
If the rates are square balanced, every compatibility class contains at most \(2n-1\) positive
equilibria and at most \(n\) sinks, and there are square-balanced rates and totals with exactly
\(n\) hyperbolic sinks. Hence more than \(n\) sinks require a nonzero effective affinity on
some square.
\end{theorem}

Theorem~\ref{thm:balanced} says that a balanced cube has the capacity of a single sequential
chain, however large its \(2^n\)-vertex state space is. Balance is weaker than permutation
symmetry of the sites: it constrains only equilibrium geometry. Under the stronger symmetric
choice of rates the cube reduces \emph{exactly} to a chain, both as an ordinary differential
equation and as a Markov chain on molecule counts (Propositions~\ref{prop:lift}
and~\ref{prop:lump}). The converse of the last sentence of Theorem~\ref{thm:balanced} is
false: an unbalanced square is not sufficient for additional sinks, and an effective affinity
is a property of competing kinetic pathways, not a chemical free energy
(Section~\ref{sec:driving}).

The remaining results concern operation and cost; their scopes are summarized in
Table~\ref{tab:scope}.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Finite-molecule memory} (Theorem~\ref{thm:finite}). For the full Markov chain of
\eqref{eq:source} at system size \(\Omega\), a rational quadratic Lyapunov function at each
hyperbolic sink yields explicit bounds for the probability of leaving a neighbourhood before
time \(t\) and for failing to return to a preparation set, uniformly over declared sets of
initial counts, together with a decoder for a bounded-error readout and nonempty sets of
actual integer preparations. The bounds are sufficient, not necessary.
\item \emph{Crowding} (Theorem~\ref{thm:action}). In the family of \cite{Companion} in which
the \(2n-1\) equilibria of a chain (or of its symmetric lift to the cube) are separated by a
small parameter \(\delta\), the reaction-path action needed to leave a declared tube around a
sink is \(c\,\delta^{2n}(1+O(\delta))\), and the exit time satisfies
\(\log\tau/(\Omega\delta^{2n})\to c\) in probability when first \(\Omega\to\infty\) and then
\(\delta\to0\). Since the recovery time is of order \(\delta^{-(2n-2)}\) and the readout gap of
order \(\delta\), barrier, recovery and resolution are tied together in this family.
\item \emph{Observation} (Propositions~\ref{prop:packing} and~\ref{prop:passive}). A bounded-error readout with
\(d_{\mathrm{obs}}\) channels of range \(R_j\) and tolerance \(\varepsilon_j\) distinguishes at
most \(\prod_j(1+\lfloor R_j/2\varepsilon_j\rfloor)\) labels; a passive observation changes
neither the chemistry nor the exit law; and a bounded-gain observation cannot separate
coalescing states.
\item \emph{Finite driving} (Theorem~\ref{thm:driving}). Every finite set of hyperbolic sinks
persists in a reversible mass-action extension that satisfies detailed balance at unit
activities and is driven by one common ATP-hydrolysis potential, provided the potential is
large enough; an explicit sufficient threshold is given in Proposition~\ref{prop:robust}.
\item \emph{A certified example} (Section~\ref{sec:example}). An explicit rational three-site
source has exactly seven positive equilibria in one class, four hyperbolic sinks and three
unstable equilibria, with well separated readouts (normalized gaps above \(0.11\)). Every
claim about it is verified in exact arithmetic. Its certified sufficient resources are
nevertheless astronomically large, and Proposition~\ref{prop:obstruction} shows that in its
design family the kinase-to-substrate ratio on one retained branch is below \(7\times10^{-11}\)
whatever positive loadings are used.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What is not claimed}
The upper bound \(2^n\) is not claimed to be sharp, and the value of \(C_n\) is open for every
\(n\ge3\). The exponential construction is not economical: the number of species and of rate
constants grows like \(n2^n\), and its rates are badly conditioned. The resource figures in
Section~\ref{sec:example} are \emph{sufficient} for the stated guarantees; they are neither
estimates of the minimum resources of the example nor lower bounds for other designs, and we do
not claim that phosphorylation memory is infeasible. No writing protocol that switches
between labels is analysed; labels are prepared. Bounds on periodic or other non-equilibrium
attractors are outside the scope; such attractors exist in sequential chains with \(n\ge3\)
\cite{CompanionOsc}. Finally, the analytic theorems are not formalized in a proof assistant;
only a few algebraic identities are (Appendix~\ref{app:certificates}).

\subsection{Organization and notation}
Section~\ref{sec:geometry} parametrizes the equilibria, proves a regularity lemma in which
only the totals are perturbed, and derives \(C_n\le2^n\). Section~\ref{sec:balance} treats
square balance and the symmetric lift. Section~\ref{sec:lower} proves the exponential lower
bound. Section~\ref{sec:finite} contains the finite-molecule theorem, Section~\ref{sec:crowding}
the crowding and observation results, Section~\ref{sec:driving} the reversible extension and
the robustness estimate, and Section~\ref{sec:example} the certified example.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses interpretation and open problems.
Appendix~\ref{app:sequential} recalls what is used from \cite{Companion},
Appendix~\ref{app:action} proves Theorem~\ref{thm:action}, and
